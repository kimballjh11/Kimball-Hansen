Context

I founded a residential lawn and landscape business in Minneapolis in 2016, at twelve years old, and ran it until August 2023. It started the way these always start: a mower, a few neighbors, and no idea what I was doing.

Lawn care has the lowest barrier to entry of any business I am aware of. Anyone with equipment and a ride can undercut you by Saturday. There is no product moat, no switching cost worth the name, and from the customer's side the only visible difference between one provider and the next is price. That sounds like a reason not to bother. It is actually the reason the business is worth studying: when the offer cannot be differentiated, everything depends on the operations behind it.

The mowing was never the hard part. The hard part was scheduling density and keeping churn near zero, and I learned both by getting them wrong first.

What I did

Pricing came first, and it was wrong for the first two years. I charged by the hour, which quietly punishes you for getting better: every improvement in speed cut my own revenue. Moving to per-property pricing, quoted from measured lot size, edging length, and obstacle count, fixed the incentive and made quoting fast enough to do on the spot.

Then routing. A client twenty minutes outside the cluster can be profitable on paper and still lose money once you count the drive. I started refusing work that did not fit the route and deliberately growing inside neighborhoods I already served, which is slower in the first season and far better in the third. Density, not headcount, is what makes this business work.

From there: negotiating and managing seasonal service contracts with 50-plus high-net-worth residential clients, then hiring and managing help once the route outgrew what I could cut myself. The last structural change was the best one. I built a winter estate maintenance line for clients who spent the cold months elsewhere, which converted a seasonal business with a dead half-year into a year-round one and gave the same clients a reason to stay on the books through the winter.

Result

The business cleared $200,000 in lifetime revenue and $50,000 in its final year, on a client base that was almost entirely repeat. Retention held around 90 percent, which is the number I am most proud of, because in a business where a competitor can undercut you every spring, a client who stays is the only asset you actually own.

I stopped operating it in August 2023. It was profitable when it ended, and it ended because I was leaving for two years, not because it had run out of room.

What I would do differently

I hired far too late. I spent at least a full season as the binding constraint on my own business, turning down work I could have taken if I had trained someone six months earlier. I told myself it was about quality control. It was mostly about not wanting to hand anything over.

I also underpriced for years out of fear of losing clients to whoever was cheapest, and the retention numbers make it obvious in hindsight that I had far more pricing room than I used. The clients were not staying because I was cheap.

The last one is the real lesson: almost nothing was written down. Routes, pricing rules, and client preferences lived in my head, which meant the business could not run a week without me and had nothing to sell at the end. I build documentation into everything now, and that is why.
