Context

Chick-fil-A franchises are owned and run by operators, which makes each one its own small business with its own P&L and its own set of unforced errors. I spent the summer of 2026 as a strategic growth intern at a Minneapolis location, reporting to the operator.

Three things were visibly leaking. Catering was underperforming what the trade area should support, and nobody could say which neighborhoods it was losing, because catering was managed on instinct and on whoever called in. Mobile app adoption was below what the market could carry, and the rewards offers driving it were national, generic, and largely ignored locally. And every store in the system was buying single-use table markers, using them once, and throwing them away.

None of that needed a new strategy. It needed someone to sit with data the store already had and act on what it said, which is most of what a growth role at this scale actually is.

What I did

The catering work started with 36 months of mobile order history. I mapped every order to its delivery ZIP and looked for the mismatch: ZIP codes with heavy individual order density and almost no catering volume. Those are not cold markets. Those are markets that already like the brand and have never been asked. That list, rather than a general radius around the store, became the outreach target, and the catering push was built on top of it.

The table marker was a cost problem rather than a growth one. I designed a reusable metal marker to replace the single-use version, specified it to survive a restaurant's daily handling, and made the case on the arithmetic of a consumable bought by every store in the system. It was picked up and rolled out across multiple markets.

On the digital side I designed a market-specific personalized reward program in place of generic national offers, tied to what local customers were actually ordering. I also built a hybrid operations app for the store covering FAQs, donation requests, market news, and a staff portal, then documented and handed it off so it would not die when my badge did. It is still in use.

Result

Catering revenue rose 47 percent over six weeks, roughly $105,000 on an annualized basis, from outreach aimed at the ZIP codes the order data had flagged. The personalized reward program drove about $60,000 in annualized incremental app revenue. The reusable table marker removed roughly $500,000 a year in national sticker cost once it rolled out beyond the store.

The operations app is the result I am least able to quantify and most confident about. It was still running after the internship ended, which is the only durable test of whether an intern's project was worth building. Most are not: they work while their author is in the building and are quietly abandoned within a month.

What I would do differently

I did not hold out a control. The catering lift is real, but the outreach ran in every flagged ZIP at once, so I cannot cleanly separate my targeting from seasonality or from the store's other activity that summer. Leaving even three ZIPs untouched would have cost almost nothing and made the number defensible instead of merely true.

I also designed the table marker before I had spoken to enough operators about how markers actually go missing. The economics held up, but I got to them by reasoning about unit cost rather than by asking the people who handle the things every day.

The handoff on the operations app was adequate rather than good. It survived because the team liked it, not because I documented it well enough to guarantee that it would.
