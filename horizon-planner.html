Product and Strategy · Daybreak Collaborative · 2026

Horizon Planner

A career diagnostic and roadmapping web app that took a consultancy's best advice out of one-on-one calls and put it in front of anyone who needed it.

100+

Users in the launch month

Context

Daybreak Collaborative is a social impact consultancy. Careers in that sector do not have the legible ladder that finance and consulting do. The same title means different things at a foundation, a nonprofit, and an impact fund, and the path from one role to the next travels mostly by word of mouth. People early in the sector ask the same handful of questions, and they ask them of whoever will pick up the phone.

Daybreak had good answers. The problem was where those answers lived: in the founder's head, handed out one conversation at a time. That does not scale, it does not compound, and a small firm cannot bill for it at any sensible rate.

The brief was to turn the advice into something a person could use without a call. Not a course and not a PDF. A tool: someone describes where they are, what they can and cannot move on, and where they want to end up, and gets back a sequenced plan instead of a list of links. I was brought in to scope it, and stayed on to design the logic and build it.

What I did

Scoping came first. I interviewed the founder to pull the advice out into something written down, then talked to people in the target group to find out which questions they were actually stuck on. The two lists were not the same. The founder talked about long-run positioning; users wanted to know what to do in the next three months. That gap set the shape of the product.

From there I defined the flow: a short intake, a diagnostic that places someone honestly rather than flatteringly, and a roadmap sequenced into near, middle, and long term. I designed the recommendation logic as an explicit rule set mapping background, constraints, and goals onto ordered steps, and kept the rules readable so the founder could revise them without coming back to me. A model that only its author can maintain is a liability disguised as an asset.

Then I built it. React and Next.js, version-controlled in Git, deployed and handed over. I wrote the question set, implemented the scoring, and worked through the edge cases that break this kind of tool: career changers with no sector experience, people optimizing for geography over role, and anyone whose stated goal did not match their stated constraints.

Result

More than 100 people used the tool in its launch month. For a small firm with no paid distribution, that was the number that mattered: it showed the demand was real and that the advice traveled without the founder attached to it.

The rest of the outcome is qualitative, and I would rather say so than dress it up. Daybreak now has an asset instead of a repeated conversation, and a version of its thinking that it can point clients and applicants toward. No cohort has been tracked long enough yet to say anything credible about whether the plans changed what people did. When that data exists, it belongs here.

What I would do differently

I designed the recommendation logic before I had enough real inputs in front of me. Testing a paper version on ten people first would have surfaced the edge cases in a week instead of in code.

I also over-scoped the first release. Several features were built because they were obvious, not because anyone asked for them, and the launch slipped for it. A narrower version shipped earlier would have taught me more.

The clearest miss is instrumentation. I did not build enough tracking to know what people do after they see their roadmap, which is precisely the question worth answering. That is a decision I would reverse on day one of the next build.
